# A Proportionate, Outcome-Based Risk Management Framework for a Small Critical Organisation

**Author:** Benjamin Webb  
**Date:** July 2026  
**Category:** Information Security / Risk Management

## Abstract

This paper describes the design of a bespoke, proportionate risk management framework for a small, regulated UK organisation operating in a critical national infrastructure context. The framework deliberately moved away from traditional scoring matrices and heat maps toward an outcome-based, judgement-led model focused on three corporate risks. Design philosophy, key components, and practical lessons are presented.

## 1. Introduction

Smaller organisations frequently struggle with traditional enterprise risk management approaches. Detailed control libraries, numerical scoring systems, and extensive risk registers can create bureaucratic overhead that exceeds the organisation’s capacity and adds little real value.

The organisation in question required a risk framework that was credible to its Board and regulators, yet remained proportionate to its size and operating model. The majority of operational delivery was performed by two key suppliers, and most Executive Committee members also acted as subject-matter experts within the framework.

## 2. Design Philosophy

Four principles guided the design:

- **Outcome-based** – Focus on preventing defined corporate risk outcomes rather than maintaining exhaustive control inventories.
- **Judgement-led** – Rely on professional judgement and early warning signals instead of numerical scoring.
- **Signal-led** – Use structured “Working Views” to surface emerging concerns from internal teams and key suppliers.
- **Proportionate** – Avoid unnecessary bureaucracy while still providing clear accountability and auditability.

## 3. The Three Corporate Risks

The framework centred on three corporate risks:

1. Hub / Service Operation Failure  
2. Legal, Compliance & Regulatory Failure  
3. Data Protection & Information Security Failure  

These risks were defined with narrative appetite statements rather than numerical thresholds. Positions relative to appetite were assessed through structured discussion and evidence rather than automated scoring.

## 4. Core Framework Components

### 4.1 End-to-End Process
A six-stage process was defined: Identify → Assess → Control → Manage → Escalate → Close.  
Language at the identification stage deliberately used “concerns” rather than “risks” to lower the barrier for raising issues.

### 4.2 Working Views
Structured Working Views were created for key suppliers and internal domains. These acted as early-warning mechanisms, capturing signals that might indicate movement toward one of the three corporate risk outcomes.

### 4.3 Roles and Ownership
Clear SME ownership was assigned for each risk domain. The Risk Coordinator role was defined as facilitative rather than owning assessments or logging concerns on behalf of others.

### 4.4 Supporting Documentation
A lean set of documents was produced, including:

- Risk Management Definitions & Principles  
- Risk Operating Model  
- Corporate Risk Oversight Process  
- Risk Appetite & Interpretation  
- Supplier Engagement & Assurance  
- End-to-End Risk Management Process  
- Operational Guide  

## 5. Key Design Decisions

- No heat maps or numerical scoring matrices were used.  
- Suppliers were trusted to operate controls, but the relevant SME was required to confirm that each control was appropriate for the risk it addressed.  
- Closure of a risk required genuine elimination of the root cause; most risks were expected to remain under ongoing management.  
- The framework was designed to be reviewed annually rather than treated as fixed.

## 6. Challenges and Lessons

### Challenges
- Directors more familiar with traditional ERM tools sometimes expected heat maps and scores.  
- Maintaining discipline around “concerns” versus formal risks required ongoing reinforcement.  
- Ensuring suppliers understood their role in the Working View and concern-logging process took time.

### Lessons Learned
1. Proportionate design is more sustainable than scaled-down traditional ERM.  
2. Narrative appetite statements can be more useful than numerical thresholds for Board-level discussion.  
3. Clear role definitions (especially the distinction between Coordinator and SME) reduce confusion.  
4. Early-warning mechanisms (Working Views) provide more value than static risk registers in a small organisation.  
5. Documentation should support the process rather than become an end in itself.

## 7. Conclusion

The outcome-based risk management framework provided a credible, usable approach tailored to the realities of a small organisation with significant third-party dependencies. By focusing on a small number of corporate risks, structured early-warning signals, and clear ownership, the framework supported effective governance without excessive bureaucracy.

The experience demonstrates that risk management can be both rigorous and proportionate when design choices are driven by organisational context rather than generic templates.
