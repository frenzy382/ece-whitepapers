# From Audit Finding to Operational Process: Creating a Supplier Cyber Incident Register

**Author:** Benjamin Webb  
**Date:** July 2026  
**Category:** Information Security / Incident Management

## Abstract

This paper describes the design and implementation of a dedicated Supplier Cyber Incident Register within a large UK infrastructure organisation. The register was created in response to an internal audit finding and the need for a clear, auditable mechanism to capture, track, and manage cyber security incidents involving third-party suppliers. The design approach, key fields, ownership model, and practical lessons are presented.

## 1. Introduction

When a cyber security incident involves a third-party supplier, responsibility and visibility can become fragmented between Information Security, Commercial/Procurement, and the supplier itself. Without a single source of truth, organisations risk incomplete records, delayed response, and weak audit evidence.

Following an internal audit observation, the organisation identified the need for a structured register specifically focused on supplier-related cyber incidents. The goal was to create a simple, maintainable tool that would support both operational response and management reporting.

## 2. Design Objectives

The register needed to:

- Capture material cyber incidents involving suppliers in a consistent format
- Provide clear ownership and status tracking
- Support both immediate response and longer-term trend analysis
- Integrate with existing incident management and continuous monitoring processes
- Remain lightweight enough to be maintained without significant overhead
- Generate useful evidence for internal audit and management review

## 3. Register Design

A simple spreadsheet-based register was chosen for speed of implementation and ease of use. Core fields included:

- Incident reference (SCR-xxx)
- Date identified / date of incident
- Supplier name and risk tier
- Short description of the incident
- Impact assessment (confidentiality, integrity, availability)
- Current status (Open / Under Investigation / Contained / Closed)
- Information Security owner
- Commercial/Procurement owner
- Actions taken and outstanding actions
- Link to continuous monitoring rating (if applicable)
- Closure date and lessons learned summary

The numbering convention (SCR-001, SCR-002, etc.) was deliberately simple to allow rapid logging.

## 4. Key Design Decisions

### 4.1 Scope Definition
Only material incidents were recorded. Minor events that did not meet agreed thresholds were handled through normal operational channels to avoid register bloat.

### 4.2 Dual Ownership
Each entry recorded both an Information Security owner and a Commercial/Procurement owner. This reflected the reality that effective supplier incident management requires collaboration between the two functions.

### 4.3 Linkage to Continuous Monitoring
Where a supplier was already enrolled in the continuous monitoring platform, the register entry referenced the relevant rating and any observed score changes. This created a connection between reactive incident handling and proactive monitoring.

### 4.4 Closure Criteria
An incident could only be marked Closed when both technical containment and any required contractual or commercial follow-up had been completed, and a short lessons-learned note had been recorded.

## 5. Implementation Approach

The register was introduced alongside the wider supplier assurance operating model. Initial entries were populated from known recent incidents and from signals generated by the continuous monitoring pilot. Ownership was assigned at the point of logging, and a simple weekly review rhythm was established to keep statuses current.

## 6. Challenges and Mitigations

- **Risk of under-reporting** – Addressed by linking the register to the continuous monitoring process and making logging a formal step in the incident playbook.
- **Maintaining momentum** – A short weekly check was more effective than relying on ad-hoc updates.
- **Avoiding duplication** with the corporate incident management system – The register was positioned as a supplier-focused overlay rather than a replacement for the main incident process.

## 7. Lessons Learned

1. **Start simple.** A well-structured spreadsheet that people actually use is more valuable than a sophisticated but neglected tool.
2. **Dual ownership improves accountability.** Recording both security and commercial owners reduces the chance of incidents falling between teams.
3. **Link reactive and proactive processes.** Connecting the incident register to continuous monitoring creates a more coherent overall picture.
4. **Define closure clearly.** Without explicit closure criteria, registers tend to accumulate stale open items.
5. **Review rhythm matters.** Even a lightweight weekly check significantly improves data quality.

## 8. Conclusion

The Supplier Cyber Incident Register provided a practical, low-overhead mechanism for tracking third-party cyber incidents in a consistent and auditable way. By focusing on material events, dual ownership, and clear linkage to broader monitoring activities, it filled an important gap identified by internal audit and supported more effective management of supplier cyber risk.
